How it works

Your conversations start
on your devices.

DoubleSlash connects people by invitation. Your app keeps your identity and conversation history. When a direct connection needs help, a server you choose can carry the conversation.

The app does the work. A server can help.

There is no central DoubleSlash account or public contact directory. You choose whom to connect to and which helper servers to use. A helper server is called a supernode.

An invitation starts the connection.

  1. Create an identity in the app.Your device makes the keys that identify you. A local passphrase protects them.
  2. Share an invite link or QR code.The receiving app checks the signed invitation and the connection handshake before saving the contact. A personal invite is single-use, lasts 15 minutes and stops working if the device that made it restarts.
  3. Use the features both apps support.The apps agree on available features, then send chat, calls or files over a direct connection or an available relay. A helper connection alone does not make someone a trusted contact.

What happens when you open an invite on the website?

The invitation is in the part of the link after the #, which the browser does not send in its web request. The page reads it locally and opens the installed app. The app checks it; the preview on the website is not proof that an invite is valid.

A saved hostname can help find a supernode after its address changes. DoubleSlash still asks the server to prove it holds the expected identity key. Optional DDNS is an address aid; you can use your own DNS or a compatible lookup service.

A helper carries private content in locked form.

What stays private from the helper

Participant chat, file contents, voice, video and shared audio are encrypted between clients. A supernode on that path does not receive the keys needed to open that content.

What the helper can see

Who connects, room membership, room names, file-offer details, timing and how much data moves. Private content does not make the connection anonymous.

Some connection-setup messages use an unencrypted WebSocket link. Proving the server's identity does not hide that setup traffic. Relayed one-to-one content uses long-lived keys: if those keys are later stolen, saved traffic can be exposed. Recipients can also keep or record what you send them.

Rooms bring a group together.

The supernode keeps track of who is in the room and forwards content. Members obtain a room key from an elected participant; the supernode does not choose or hold it. A private room only lets you in with a valid invitation or membership permission.

Your app saves the room definition and can recreate it after a server restart. The server keeps room hosting in memory: user-created rooms disappear after about 15 minutes with no voice participants or text subscribers. Conversation history remains on each participant's device.

Several servers can form one cluster, shown as one supernode in the app. They pass room traffic between them and provide alternate connection routes if a member goes down. They do not keep a conversation archive for people who were offline.

Leaving voice keeps the text room selected. Hide keeps a room out of this device's list; on desktop it also leaves its voice session. Remove removes the saved room across your devices and can delete this device's history. Other people keep their room and their copies.

Calls travel live. Room files wait for a request.

Voice and video

Live media uses a fast encrypted connection. If a direct call cannot connect, it can use a temporary private room through an available trusted supernode. Video quality adapts to connection pressure; shared audio plays separately from microphone audio.

Files

In a room, an offer tells people a file is available. Accepting downloads it from the sender, who must remain reachable. Large files stream from disk, with a limit of 4 GiB. Withdrawing an offer stops further serving but cannot erase downloaded copies.

A whole-screen share includes notifications and pop-ups. On older Windows versions, choosing application audio can fall back to sharing all computer audio. Check what you are sharing before starting.

Your data stays with your devices.

Each device keeps its own history and received files. An encrypted backup can carry your identity, saved contacts, rooms and available attachments to a new profile. Keep its password safe: the backup and password can recover your identity, and there is no central account-recovery service.

Devices using the same imported identity have routing support, live Message myself text, and room-definition/removal sharing. They do not continuously copy chat history. Ordinary file-based moves still mean quitting the source before connecting the destination; automatic device pairing and full simultaneous-use acceptance are unfinished.

A supernode can also host pages inside DoubleSlash.

Portals and small shared apps open in the native client and use its connection, rather than a public game web server. The bundled demos keep shared state in open pages; closing the last page loses it.

The operator supplies that page's code. These apps do not automatically inherit private-chat encryption: game data is forwarded without encryption against the relay unless the app adds it. Only open hosted content you trust.

You can run your own supernode. Desktop setup can manage a rented Linux server over SSH, but its security, backups, availability and hosting bill remain your responsibility.

What still needs work?

The connection, chat, room, file and media paths described here are implemented. That is different from every setup being tested on real hardware.

  • Windows has camera, screen/window and shared-audio capture. Complete two-client and room-media acceptance is still open.
  • Linux and macOS have camera backends awaiting hardware validation, without screen or shared-audio capture.
  • Android sends camera video. Opt-in incoming video and shared-audio playback are implemented but still need physical-device acceptance; screen and shared-audio capture are not built.
  • Additional server-setup, IPv6-only relay and changed-address recovery checks remain open. Source code alone does not establish a hosted service's current health.
